The strongest local diagnostic varies by domain, producing a per-domain envelope with macro F1 0.392 rather than one runnable system.
Compared with that envelope, the F1 gains are 0.420 (Code), 0.267 (SmartHome), 0.161 (Shopping), 0.188 (Email), 0.264 (Calendar), and 0.265 (Project Management).
The selected baselines produce 604 false positives and 822 pushes, whereas \system{} produces 176 false positives and 408 pushes, reductions of 70.9\% and 50.4\%, respectively.

The external conditions expose a different failure mode.
Mem0-backed yields 141 TP, 417 FP, 160 FN, and 2,717 TN (558 pushes; micro F1 0.328).
ProactiveAgent-Prompt yields 189 TP, 1,143 FP, 112 FN, and 1,991 TN (1,332 pushes; micro F1 0.231).
The latter recovers 62.8\% of positive turns but has micro precision 0.142, so its low F1 primarily reflects over-proposal under the frozen proposal/null policy.
This result characterizes the prompt adaptation, not the released trained ProactiveAgent system; similarly, the Mem0 row characterizes our memory-backed decision interface rather than all Mem0 configurations.

The study-built Necessity Verifier is applied only to non-null proposals and is distinct from ProactiveAgent's learned evaluator.
It removes 573 of 731 false positives (78.4\%) but also loses 52 of 127 true positives (40.9\%), raising micro F1 from 0.253 to 0.396 while lowering recall from 0.870 to 0.514.
It adds a model call and cannot recover stage-one abstentions, so it is not an equal-compute baseline.
The approximate cooldown changes the operating point but does not close the gap to \system{}; because it lacks native per-habit keys, it does not isolate cooldown as the sole cause.

A separate frozen profile-shuffle control tests profile ownership on 30 sessions and 479 paired turns.
The aligned arm reaches F1 0.600 and the shuffled arm 0.000, a paired session-bootstrap difference of 0.600 (95\% CI $[0.444,0.719]$); the aggregate exact McNemar test is nonsignificant ($p=0.568$).
Every shuffled profile abstains because donor trigger keys do not match, so this verifies dependence on the assigned profile rather than fine-grained semantic discrimination, and it does not replace the continual Full result.

\textbf{Generalized matching.}
Full exceeds Exact-only in Shopping, Calendar, Email, and Project Management, with session-bootstrap intervals excluding zero: $[0.061,0.298]$, $[0.351,0.700]$, $[0.292,0.668]$, and $[0.311,0.677]$, respectively.
Code and SmartHome are exact negative controls with identical predictions.
Thus abstraction is most useful when preferences or workflow entities vary semantically, not when normalized events already match literally.

\textbf{Phase gate.}
Removing the gate lowers Code F1 by 0.205 (95\% CI $[0.131,0.276]$) and increases FP from 23 to 59.
The effect is repeatable across three runs, while four non-Code domains are prediction-identical with and without the gate.

\textbf{Think.}
Code w/o Think has a three-run mean F1 of 0.659 versus 0.667 for Full, and two repeats are prediction-identical to Full.
We therefore do not claim a robust aggregate contribution from the optional LLM checker.

\textbf{Cooldown.}
Removing Shopping cooldown raises turn-level F1 from 0.600 to 0.701 by increasing recall, but pushes rise from 58 to 85 and false positives from 25 to 37.
At cooldown three, 27 candidates are suppressed: 15 potential true positives and 12 potential false positives.
Cooldown therefore encodes an intervention-cost policy rather than an accuracy-only improvement.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

Errors concentrate at the boundary between retrieving a related habit and deciding that it is actionable.
In the Code Hard subset, 11/17 false positives occur in aligned or exception negatives, while other errors push on early edits and miss the valid wrap-up stage.
The Shopping Hard subset similarly contains premature pushes before final decision points and false positives on preference-aligned actions.
In Calendar-all, at least 20/33 false positives are directly verified as aligned or exception cases.

These errors identify three remaining weaknesses.
First, stage cues outside Code are less explicit, so relevant habits can fire before an actionable decision.
Second, a related preference is sometimes mistaken for a violated preference.
Third, implicit semantic evidence is weaker than explicit normalized conflict keys.
The findings support the architectural separation in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}: habit discovery can succeed while push control still fails.
